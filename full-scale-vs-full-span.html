Measurement terminology

Full scale vs. full span

The terms can produce the same number on a zero-based range, which is why they are often treated as interchangeable. On compound, vacuum, or suppressed-zero ranges, they can be very different — and that difference can change how an accuracy specification is calculated.

SHORT VERSION

Full scale commonly refers to the maximum or upper value of a range. Full span is the algebraic difference between the upper and lower range values. But the abbreviation FS is not universal: some manufacturers use it for “full scale,” while others explicitly use it for “full span.” Always check the definition in the specification you are reading.

The two definitions

Full scale

Usually the upper endpoint

In many pressure specifications, full scale means the maximum indicated or upper range value. A 0 to 100 psi instrument therefore has a full-scale value of 100 psi.

Full span

Upper value minus lower value

Span is the algebraic difference between the upper and lower limits of the range. It describes the total width of the measurement interval.

Full span = upper range value − lower range value

When are they the same?

For a range that starts at zero, the maximum value and the span are numerically identical. That makes the terminology easy to overlook.

Instrument rangeUpper valueFull spanSame?
0 to 100 psi100 psi100 psiYes
−15 to +30 psi30 psi45 psiNo

ASME B40.100 describes span as the algebraic difference between the limits of the range and gives the same type of compound-range example: a −15 psi to +30 psi gauge has a span of 45 psi.

Why “%FS” can be ambiguous

The letters FS do not have one guaranteed meaning across every datasheet. Fluke Calibration documentation, for example, defines % FS as percent of full scale and identifies full scale with the maximum indication, while separately defining span as maximum minus minimum pressure range. Other documentation explicitly defines FS as Full Span.

Practical rule:

Never calculate an accuracy specification from “%FS” until you have found the manufacturer’s definition of FS for that product.

Why the definition matters for accuracy

Consider an instrument with a range of −15 to +30 psi and an accuracy specification of ±0.1% FS.

If FS means full-scale / maximum value

The reference value is 30 psi:

0.1% × 30 psi = ±0.030 psi
If FS means full span

The span is 30 − (−15) = 45 psi:

0.1% × 45 psi = ±0.045 psi

That is a 50% difference in the calculated error limit even though the printed specification still says “±0.1% FS.” The definition matters most when the lower end of the range is not zero.

How to read the specification correctly

  1. Find the stated range. Identify the lower and upper range values.
  2. Find the definition of FS. Check the datasheet notes, specifications table, manual, or glossary.
  3. Calculate span when needed. Subtract the lower value from the upper value algebraically.
  4. Convert the percentage to engineering units. Multiply the specified percentage by the quantity the manufacturer defines.
  5. Check for additional terms. Many instruments combine %FS or %span with % of reading, resolution, temperature, or other contributions.

Related terms

Range

The interval of values an instrument is intended or configured to measure, normally described by a lower and upper limit.

Upper range value (URV)

The upper endpoint of the configured measurement range. On a 200 to 500 psi range, the URV is 500 psi.

Lower range value (LRV)

The lower endpoint of the configured measurement range. On a 200 to 500 psi range, the LRV is 200 psi.

Percent of reading

An error term based on the actual measured value rather than a fixed range quantity. Unlike a pure %FS or %span term, its absolute contribution changes as the reading changes.
