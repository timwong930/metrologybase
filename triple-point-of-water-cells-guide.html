How can a glass cell give you one of the most reliable temperatures on Earth?

Triple point of water (TPW) cells use a rare coexistence of ice, liquid water, and water vapor to establish 0.01°C. Here is what makes them useful, the science of the ice mantle, what a mysterious “water hammer” tells you, and how the Additel 878-160 makes the process easier.

A thermometer reference that does not depend on a dial setting

At the triple point of water, solid ice, liquid water, and water vapor coexist in thermal equilibrium. For a properly realized reference cell with specified water composition, the ITS-90 reference temperature is 273.16 K (0.01°C). Unlike an ordinary stirred ice bath, a sealed TPW cell controls the water's own vapor environment, giving thermometry laboratories an extraordinarily reproducible fixed point.

It is not simply the temperature at which ice melts in open air. The familiar atmospheric freezing point is about 0°C; the TPW is 0.01°C at the water's equilibrium vapor pressure, roughly 611.657 Pa. The internal pressure is established by the sealed system, not by setting a gauge in the laboratory.

Why do calibration laboratories use TPW cells?

Check reference PRT/SPRT drift

Measure the probe resistance at a known fixed point, commonly recorded as RTPW. Repeated measurements help reveal changes after transport, thermal cycling, mechanical shock or aging.

Support ITS-90 thermometry

Water's triple point is a foundational fixed point used in standard platinum resistance thermometer calibration and temperature-scale realization. It is a reference, not a replacement for the full calibration procedure.

Verify your temperature reference

A stable bath or dry well still needs a trustworthy sensor. TPW measurement helps validate that sensor's assigned resistance, supporting confidence in the rest of the temperature calibration chain.

Improve repeatability and traceability

A qualified cell and a controlled procedure can give a highly reproducible check point. Traceability still requires documented instruments, comparisons or qualifications, corrections, and an uncertainty budget.

Example: A reference PRT used in a dry well reads a slightly different resistance at TPW than it did last month. That difference can reveal reference-probe drift before it affects many customer calibrations. Whether the change exceeds a tolerance depends on the probe history and the laboratory's uncertainty and acceptance rules.

The science: why all three phases make a temperature anchor

In a pressure–temperature phase diagram, boundaries show conditions where two phases coexist. Where the solid–liquid, liquid–vapor and solid–vapor boundaries meet, three phases are simultaneously in equilibrium. Heating or cooling during the phase change is absorbed or released as latent heat rather than immediately producing a large temperature change; the equilibrium fixes the reference condition.

Why the ice mantle matters

The cell is a sealed glass vessel containing high-purity, degassed water and water vapor, with a separate inner thermometer well. Cooling creates an ice mantle around that well. A very thin liquid layer adjacent to the thermometer well can be formed so the probe sees the ice–water equilibrium while the outer cell maintains the triple-phase state.

Results at microkelvin-level precision are sensitive to water isotopes, soluble contamination, liquid depth (hydrostatic head), thermometer self-heating, heat leaks, and the way the ice mantle is prepared and maintained. A TPW cell is only as good as its qualification and measurement procedure.

Important 2019 correction: The TPW formerly defined the kelvin. Since the 2019 SI redefinition, the kelvin is defined through a fixed value of the Boltzmann constant. TPW remains an essential ITS-90 practical reference point, not the current definition of the SI kelvin. NIST explanation ↗

The “water hammer”: an unexpected quality check

If a TPW cell makes a sharp, glass-like clink when it is carefully inverted at room temperature, that sound has a technical meaning. In the water-hammer test described in ASTM E1750, liquid rushes through the sealed vessel and the water-vapor pocket collapses as the liquid strikes the end of the cell. If noncondensable air is present, it cushions the impact and the sound becomes duller. This gives a useful qualitative check for air contamination or compromised cell integrity.

Sharp “clink”

Consistent with a well-evacuated cell: minimal noncondensable gas allows a more abrupt vapor collapse and impact.

Duller impact

Can indicate extra air acting like a spring. It is a reason to investigate further, not a standalone verdict on calibration accuracy.

Handle with care: Water-hammer integrity testing is a controlled procedure for the appropriate cell type and temperature, not an invitation to strike or vigorously shake glassware. Follow the cell maker's instructions and ASTM E1750, and use suitable protection and handling methods.

Water hammer is not the same as Additel's “shake” step

These are often confused. Water hammer checks for the presence of noncondensable air through the collapse/impact sound, usually at room temperature. Shaking a supercooled TPW cell during realization promotes ice nucleation so freezing starts. The Additel procedure uses the second phenomenon; it is not a substitute for the integrity test.

For formal handling and integrity procedures, consult ASTM E1750-23.

Two ways to realize the triple point

Traditional laboratory method

  1. Inspect and qualify the cell, including integrity checks.
  2. Cool the thermometer well to form a controlled ice mantle around it.
  3. Condition/anneal the mantle using the cell and lab procedure.
  4. Create a thin melt layer next to the well and insert the thermometer.
  5. Read resistance after equilibrium; apply relevant corrections.

Reference: NIST preparation demonstration. The long annealing procedure NIST describes is not identical to rapid supercooling methods.

Automated supercooling method

  1. Place the compatible cell assembly in a controlled instrument.
  2. Run the device's programmed cool-down sequence.
  3. At the prompt, carefully remove and agitate the supercooled cell to initiate freezing.
  4. Return the cell to the instrument to maintain the triple point.
  5. Measure and record PRT resistance at TPW.

Reference: Additel TPW-kit datasheet.

What TPW cells cannot tell you on their own

  • One point is not a complete multi-temperature calibration. A good RTPW check is especially valuable for drift surveillance, but it does not alone verify the full response of a PRT across its range.
  • Cell qualification is separate from routine thermometer calibration. ASTM E1750 distinguishes demonstrating a valid fixed point from calibrating the thermometer placed in it.
  • Uncertainty is method-specific. A datasheet's microkelvin figure does not eliminate readout accuracy, self-heating, hydrostatic head, contamination, thermal gradients or probe effects.
  • Handle glass and thermal fluids carefully. Incorrect fluid use, rough handling or unauthorized steps risk breakage and measurement error.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the TPW 0.01°C instead of 0°C?

Because the triple point occurs at the equilibrium water-vapor pressure in a sealed cell, not atmospheric pressure. The distinction is small in degrees but important in precision thermometry.

Does hearing a water hammer prove the cell is accurate?

No. The sound is a qualitative integrity clue; documented cell qualification, water composition, contamination checks and proper realization still matter.

Can I use an ordinary ice bath instead?

An ice bath can be excellent for many practical checks near 0°C, but it does not automatically reproduce the TPW's intrinsic sealed three-phase reference or its achievable uncertainty.

Do I need the process calibrator (PC) option on the 878?
